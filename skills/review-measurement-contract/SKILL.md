# Review the measurement contract

Find what a result actually establishes and the smallest additional evidence needed for the user's decision. Perform the requested review or report; do not start new experiments merely because they could help. Answer the decision question directly, including "not yet decidable" when the evidence does not support a verdict.

1. Identify the decision, target population, unit of analysis, outcome, and reference/label definition. Distinguish an observed proxy from the real outcome of interest. Keep completed work, interpretation, and planned work separate.
2. Trace the compared results to their source artifacts and versions. Check input membership, exclusions, and missing cases; denominator and aggregation; labels; time window/alignment; preprocessing; effective runtime configuration; compute/model capacity; and measurement procedure where relevant. A command-line setting may be overridden: prefer the resolved configuration and actual execution receipt. When the source is silent about one side's configuration, mark it unknown rather than assuming parity. Mark differences and unknowns explicitly, without demanding irrelevant fields.
3. Recompute derived figures from documented raw values when sufficient information is available. For counts and rates, retain both numerators and denominators. If the claim compares methods on the same cases but one excluded cases, rebuild the matched subset where possible and separately retain the excluded population; matching does not establish full-population performance. Different sample sizes alone do not invalidate comparisons between independently sampled comparable populations. For continuous measurements, check units, coverage, and aggregation rather than forcing a count-rate format. Distinguish an observed difference from its uncertainty and causal attribution: small samples alone prove neither significance nor no effect. Do not invent missing data or intervals; when statistical analysis is requested and adequate data are available, compute it with a stated method and assumptions, otherwise identify what remains unknown.
4. Decide which comparisons remain meaningful. If a contract changed, explain which old conclusion no longer transfers, why, and what narrower comparison still stands. Do not silently combine the best values from incompatible runs or erase historical results.
5. Check whether the baseline is informative for the proposed benefit. Prefer a cheap, credible alternative and comparable capacity/budget when these affect the claim. Separate the effect of the added technique from simultaneous changes. Better proxy agreement, offline replay, or unit tests do not by themselves establish real-world success.
6. Report supported findings, unresolved discrepancies, and the next smallest check with its decision purpose. Attach counts and uncertainty where available; no detected difference does not prove equivalence. Missing evidence is not evidence of failure.

For progress reporting, cover the relevant work streams instead of expanding one favorable experiment. Do not count plans or requests as delivered work. If reporting completion fractions, establish the actual work-item set, distinguish issues from PRs, name items missing from the denominator, and note overlapping categories. Do not reconstruct historical scope from current membership without history. Do not report an item as completed or in progress without supporting source evidence.

Report preparation separately from usable outcomes. A completed checklist, short smoke run or accepted plan is not a completed training run, evaluation or delivered product. Use actual artifact paths, run state and measured results. Do not turn arbitrary checklist counts into an overall percentage; use a stable outcome denominator when one exists, otherwise report stages without a percentage. Derive estimates from observed throughput and remaining work, with uncertainty. State when the first useful artifact has not yet been produced, even if preparation is extensive.

Distinguish a result-invalidating discrepancy from a receipt or wording error. Fix the latter without blocking otherwise valid execution or rerunning unchanged work. For a substantive discrepancy, identify the affected conclusion/check and the smallest correction; do not invalidate unrelated evidence.

Before finishing, check the written deliverable against these points: every headline number traces to a source figure or is labelled as this review's own recomputation, each stated comparison identifies its population and coverage (and denominators for rates), and every excluded or unknown item is still visible rather than dropped for a cleaner story. Fix what fails in place. That a write succeeded is not evidence the document is usable.

Adapt the output to the request: a few bullets can suffice; use a comparison table when conditions materially differ. Keep claim, evidence, limitation, and next action close together. In an existing human-edited document, preserve relevant content and explain material corrections. Do not invent times, metrics, causes, or personal reflection to populate a template. Respect the requested output shape; include only the concise calculations or notes needed to make a material claim unambiguous.
